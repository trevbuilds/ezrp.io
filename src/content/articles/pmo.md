  The PMO is the function that makes an ERP programme legible: what is being spent, what is being decided, what is slipping and who is accountable.
  It is a delivery concern rather than a business one, and confusing the two is how programmes end up governed by the people who cannot change anything.

## Why it matters

An ERP programme runs for a year or more, spends millions, and touches every part of the organisation. Without a function holding the plan, the money, the decisions and the risks in one place, each workstream optimises for itself and the integration cost surfaces at testing.

- **It holds the single plan**: One schedule across streams, so a dependency between payroll and the ledger is visible before it bites.
- **It holds the decision record**: Decisions made once, recorded, and not relitigated every steering committee.
- **It holds the truth about progress**: Status that reflects what is finished, not what is nearly finished.

## The workflow

```steps
Set up :: Governance bodies, delegations, cadence and the artefacts each one owns, agreed before mobilisation.
Plan :: One integrated schedule across workstreams, with dependencies named rather than implied.
Track :: Progress, spend, risk, issue and decision status collected on a fixed cadence.
Report :: Each forum given what it can act on — steering decides, working groups resolve.
Control :: Change requests, budget movements and scope decisions handled through one route.
Assure :: Independent checkpoints at gates, testing the evidence rather than the confidence.
Close :: Benefits handed to owners, lessons recorded, and the programme actually stood down.
```

> A PMO that only collects status is an overhead. A PMO that owns the dependency map, the decision log and the change route is the thing that keeps the programme finishable.

## What you need in place

- **A delegation instrument for the programme**: What the programme director can approve, what the steering committee must, and what goes to the board.
- **A single integrated plan**: Not one plan per workstream stapled together.
- **One change route**: Scope, budget and schedule changes assessed the same way regardless of who raises them.
- **A risk and issue register with owners**: Risks without named owners are observations.
- **Definition of done per deliverable**: Agreed before work starts, or status is opinion.
- **Independent assurance at gates**: Someone whose job is not to deliver the programme.

## Questions to ask

```qa
Authority :: What can the programme decide without going to the business? :: Ambiguity here produces either paralysis or decisions the business does not accept.
Single plan :: Is there one schedule, or one per workstream? :: Cross-stream dependencies live in the gaps between separate plans.
Status honesty :: How is percent complete determined? :: If it is self-reported effort, it will be ninety percent for months.
Change route :: Can scope change without a change request? :: It always can informally; the question is whether anyone sees it.
Assurance :: Who checks the programme independently, and who do they report to? :: Assurance reporting to the programme director is not assurance.
Closure :: What has to be true for this programme to end? :: Programmes without an end condition become operating costs.
```

## Metrics and KPIs to track

```metrics
Schedule performance :: Milestones met on the baseline date, and cumulative slip.
Budget performance :: Spend and committed against baseline, forecast to complete.
Decision latency :: Time from a decision being requested to it being made.
Open risks by severity :: With owner and mitigation currency.
Change request volume :: Raised, approved and their aggregate schedule and cost impact.
Deliverable acceptance :: Deliverables accepted first time against those reworked.
```
